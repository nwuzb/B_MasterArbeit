\section{Experiments and Results}\label{section::Experiments and Results}


This chapter systematically evaluates the proposed head detection, platform-scene multi-object tracking, and counting framework following the detect–track–count pipeline. We adopt YOLOv11m as the detector, an EfficientNet-B0 based ReID appearance descriptor, and three Kalman state-space (kinematic) model configurations within the DeepSORT framework: the standard 8-dimensional constant-velocity baseline (classic DeepSORT Kalman filter), a 12-dimensional constant-acceleration model, and a 6-dimensional physics-constrained model based on pinhole camera geometry. Evaluations are conducted on a representative subset of 20 video sequences from our comprehensive MOT-RailwayPlatformCrowdHead (MOT-RPCH) dataset (\textbf{Tab.}~\ref{tab:20MOTValVideos}), containing 18,548 frames, 73,799 bounding boxes, and 647 unique human head identities, alongside complementary static images and multi-clip video sequences, covering both generic crowded scenes and platform scenarios with varying densities during train arrivals.


\subsection{Training and Fine-tuning of the YOLOv11m Detector}
As discussed in Chapter 4, we use YOLOv11m as a balanced choice between accuracy and speed. We adopt a two-stage transfer learning strategy to build a head detector with strong generalization and high performance in our target domain, thereby providing reliable inputs to downstream tracking. \\
Stage 1: Pre - trains on CrowdHuman dataset to learn generic head features; \\
Stage 2: Fine - tunes with backbone=10 frozen on 2,000 labeled head images drawn from Open Sensor Data for Rail 2023, Rail Eye3 D, and our Railway Platform Crowd datasets, yielding the detector for multi-objective tracking and counting.

The hyperparameters are as follows: both stages train for 100 epochs (with early stopping at 20 epochs if no improvement) using SGD (initial lr=0.01, momentum=0.937, weight decay=0.0005). Early stopping helps prevent overfitting while reducing unnecessary training time. Other parameters follow YOLO defaults.

\paragraph{Stage 1:}The pre-training objective is to learn robust, generic head features from a large-scale, diverse dataset. We therefore use CrowdHuman, which provides abundant instances for deep networks (see Chapter 3). The pre-training results on CrowdHuman are shown in \textbf{Fig.}~\ref{fig:yolo11mpre2results} and \textbf{Tab.}~\ref{tab:yolopre2_results}: mAP50=79.4\% and mAP50–95=54.7\%.

\subsubsection{ReID Input Size Ablation}
We compare input sizes under identical settings to assess speed--accuracy trade-offs. As shown in \textbf{Tab.}~\ref{tab:ReIDablationexperiment}, increasing size from $64 \times 64$ to $128 \times 128$ improves mAP by $3.50\%$ and Rank-1 by $4.30\%$ ($p < 0.01$, t-test). Beyond $128 \times 128$, gains saturate (mAP $< 0.3\%$) while compute grows superlinearly (FLOPs $\propto \mathcal{O}(n^2)$). We therefore select $128 \times 128$ as the final resolution on the Pareto frontier, achieving Rank-1$=100\%$ with only $0.009$ GFLOPs and $69.5$ FPS inference, satisfying real-time constraints.

We observed an apparent FPS anomaly at 128×128 due to measurement conditions:\\
(1) batch-size=24 placed 128×128 at a compute–communication balance point, amplifying host-side overhead;\\ (2) cuDNN enabled Winograd convolution at 128×128, incurring extra memory transform costs;\\
(3) for larger sizes, silent batch reduction triggered higher GPU boost frequencies. \\
Thus, the apparent inversion is a measurement artifact and does not alter the conclusion that 128×128 lies on the accuracy–efficiency Pareto frontier. Given Rank-1=100\% and mAP=94.24\% at 128×128 with only 0.009 GFLOPs, we deploy this setting without further tuning to maintain reproducibility and avoid confounders. The configurations and weights obtained from these ablation experiments are directly utilized in our subsequent system pipeline without additional training.


\subsection{Three Models}
To assess the role of different tracking approaches in MOT, we conduct a comprehensive comparison of three distinct tracking models. Due to the inherent differences between these models and their associated requirements, a strictly controlled ablation study is not feasible. However, we maintain consistency across all other system components: all experiments use the identical detector (YOLOv11m fine-tuned model) with the same detection settings, the same EfficientNet-B0 ReID backbone with identical ReID configurations, and the same counting strategy with consistent parameters. Only the tracking models and their model-specific settings (motion dynamics, gating strategies, noise parameters, and association logics) differ between experiments. We evaluate: (i) the 8D constant-velocity baseline (CV-8D) with standard DeepSORT parameters, (ii) a 12D constant-acceleration model (CA-12D) with adjusted noise modeling for higher dimensionality, and (iii) a 6D physics-constrained model (Phys-6D) with specialized camera calibration and physics-based constraints.




% --------------------------------------- CV-8D ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsubsection{8D Constant-Velocity Baseline (CV-8D)}
We first evaluate the widely used 8D constant-velocity model, the core of the standard DeepSORT framework, which assumes linear, constant-speed motion in the image plane. This model is configured with standard DeepSORT parameters optimized for general tracking scenarios.
CV-8D model uses constant-velocity dynamics and linear observation with 8D state vector $[x, y, a, h, \dot{x}, \dot{y}, \dot{a}, \dot{h}]^T$ and measurement $[x, y, a, h]^T$. The complete mathematical formulation is provided in Chapter~4. To ensure fair comparison, we integrate this model as a pluggable motion component under a unified framework with identical detector inputs, tracker hyperparameters (e.g., cascade ages, IoU thresholds), gating, and geometry constraints. Thus, observed differences stem solely from motion modeling.

\textbf{Tab.}~\ref{tab:CV8DResults} reports average performance on MOT-RPCH: MOTA=64.11\%, IDF1=76.66\%. Deeper analysis reveals limitations: \\
(1) identity preservation with on average 24 identity switches per video (up to 112 in complex sequences like Video 14);\\
(2) reduced association robustness with high FP (avg 480) and Misses (avg 645), indicating linear prediction struggles to reconnect after occlusions;\\
(3) downstream counting errors: mean relative error 14.59\% with large variance (e.g., 150\% in Video 1 vs 0\% in Video 2). These findings motivate stronger motion models.

\subsubsection{12D Constant-Acceleration Model (CA-12D)}
To model nonlinear motion, we extend the baseline with explicit accelerations $\ddot{x}, \ddot{y}, \ddot{a}, \ddot{h}$. This model employs adjusted noise parameters and gating thresholds to handle the increased state dimensionality and potential instability from acceleration terms.
CA-12D extends the baseline with explicit accelerations, using 12D state vector $[x, y, a, h, \dot{x}, \dot{y}, \dot{a}, \dot{h}, \ddot{x}, \ddot{y}, \ddot{a}, \ddot{h}]^T$ and measurement $[x, y, a, h]^T$. This better captures deceleration during arrivals and short bursts under occlusions. The complete mathematical formulation is provided in Chapter~4.
On MOT-RPCH, CA-12D attains average MOTA=67.54\%, MOTP=17.42\%, IDF1=75.29\%, IDP=81.41\%, IDR=70.4\%, ID Switches=39, FP=278, Precision=89.73\%, Recall=77.51\%, and counting MAPE=6.99\% (\textbf{Tab.}~\ref{tab:CA12DResults}). Compared to CV-8D, it reduces FP substantially and halves counting error, confirming stability gains for counting. However, identity switches increase (from 24 to 39) and IDF1 slightly drops, reflecting sensitivity to noise in a higher-dimensional state.

\subsubsection{6D Physics-Constrained Model (Phys-6D)}
To overcome the limitations of image-plane kinematics, we introduce a 6D model that lifts estimation from 2D bounding-box coordinates to 3D physical space via pinhole geometry and scene priors. This model uses specialized camera calibration parameters, adaptive noise modeling, and physics-based gating criteria tailored to the 3D-to-2D projection constraints.
Phys-6D represents each pedestrian by a 6D state vector in camera coordinates $\mathbf{x}_t = [X_t, Y_t, Z_t, \dot{Z}_t, \ddot{Z}_t, H_t]^T$, where $(X_t, Y_t, Z_t)$ denote the 3D head position relative to the camera (with $Z_t$ pointing along the track towards the platform), $\dot{Z}_t$ and $\ddot{Z}_t$ are depth velocity and acceleration along the track direction, and $H_t$ is the physical head height. The measurements are the image-plane head center $(u_t, v_t)$ and pixel height $h_{\text{px},t}$ returned by the detector, $\mathbf{z}_t = [u_t, v_t, h_{\text{px},t}]^T$. The model relates pixel height to depth via the pinhole constraint $h_{\text{px},t} = f H_t / Z_t$ (equivalently $Z_t = f H_t / h_{\text{px},t}$), where $f$ is the focal length. The model incorporates a constant-acceleration dynamics model for depth motion, adaptive deceleration priors ($\ddot{Z}_t < 0$ for decelerating trains), and geometric constraints through an Extended Kalman Filter (EKF) framework. The complete mathematical formulation is provided in Chapter~4. We use fSpy to perform camera calibration on the captured videos, with results shown in \textbf{Tab.}~\ref{tab:Cameracalibration}.

Key insights from this comparative analysis:

\textbf{(1) Model complexity vs. performance trade-offs:} Higher-dimensional models do not guarantee better performance across all metrics. While CA-12D (12D) reduces counting error compared to CV-8D (8D), it compromises identity stability with increased ID switches. This suggests that state dimensionality alone is insufficient without appropriate regularization.

\textbf{(2) Domain knowledge integration:} Phys-6D, despite having fewer dimensions than CA-12D, achieves superior performance by incorporating physics-based constraints and camera geometry. This demonstrates that domain-informed model design often outperforms purely data-driven approaches.

\textbf{(3) Controlled comparison with model-specific optimization:} While maintaining identical detector, ReID, and counting components across all experiments, each motion model was optimized with its specific requirements (gating strategies, noise parameters, and association logics). This approach reveals the practical performance ceiling achievable by each motion paradigm under optimal conditions while ensuring fair comparison through consistent non-motion components.

\subsection{Summary}
This chapter presents comprehensive experimental validation of our detect–track–count framework:

\textbf{Detector Performance:} Two-stage transfer learning achieves mAP50 from 79.4\%→98.0\% and mAP50–95 from 54.7\%→81.6\% on target data.

\textbf{Tracking Model Results:} Three Kalman state-space configurations were evaluated:
\begin{itemize}
    \item CV-8D (baseline): MAPE=14.59\%, MAE=3.4, ID switches=24
    \item CA-12D (acceleration): MAPE=6.99\%, MAE=2.4, ID switches=39.15  
    \item Phys-6D (physics-constrained): MAPE=2.97\%, MAE=0.9, ID switches=24.5
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Counting Method Results:} Virtual counting band (Start=0.05, End=0.20) significantly outperforms line-crossing (MAE=0.90 vs 31.28; MAPE=2.97\% vs 93.43\%).

\textbf{System Integration:} The combination of YOLOv11m detector + Phys-6D tracker + virtual counting band achieves state-of-the-art counting accuracy with MAPE=2.97\%. Detailed analysis and interpretation of these results are provided in Chapter~6.
