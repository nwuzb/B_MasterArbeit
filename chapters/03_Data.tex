\section{Data}\label{section::data}
In this study, we meticulously selected four datasets, leveraging both publicly available large-scale datasets and our own self-constructed ones to achieve more efficient and accurate methods for crowd detection, tracking, and counting on railway platforms. Our key contribution is the construction of the comprehensive MOT-RailwayPlatformCrowdHead (MOT-RPCH) dataset, containing 27 video sequences with 24,788 frames, 89,087 bounding boxes, and 885 unique human head identities. For MOT evaluation, we selected a representative subset of 20 video sequences (\textbf{Tab.}~\ref{tab:20MOTValVideos}). This section presents a detailed description of each dataset used in our experiments.

\subsection{Dataset for YOLO Detector}
CrowdHuman \cite{shao2018crowdhuman} is a benchmark dataset that is large-scale, richly annotated, and highly diverse. It contains images of crowds across different scenes, illumination conditions, densities, ages, and skin tones. Each human instance is annotated with a head bounding box, a visible-region bounding box, and a full-body bounding box, aiming to address the limitations of detection models when confronting occlusion in crowded scenes. The dataset consists of 15,000 training images and 4,370 validation images. In total, there are approximately 470K human instances from the training and validation subsets, with an average of about 22–23 people per image, covering a spectrum from negative samples to high-density scenes with various occlusions. We focus on head detection, where head sizes range across pixels. We use the 15,000 training images and 4,370 validation images as the training and test sets, respectively, for the first-stage training of our YOLOv11m-size model.

\subsection{Fine-tuning Datasets for the YOLO Detector}
We fine-tune the detector using datasets that we curated and annotated. Data from three different sources were integrated and labeled; the annotated data were then used to fine-tune the model weights trained in the first stage.

 \paragraph{(1) Open Sensor Data for Rail 2023} \cite{OpenSensorDataforRail2023}. This dataset comprises \textbf{45 annotated multi-sensor sequences} (RGB cameras, infrared cameras, LiDAR, radar, positioning, IMU). Images have a resolution of \textbf{2464×1600}, and the data were collected on various rail tracks in Hamburg, Germany. We adopted video sequences captured by the RGB camera located at the middle front of the train head and extracted one image every 50 frames to reduce redundancy, resulting in \textbf{20 images} and \textbf{180 human instances}. We annotated head bounding boxes using Roboflow and incorporated these images into our second-stage fine-tuning.

As shown in \textbf{Fig.}~\ref{fig:opensensor-samples-zh}, we present sample images from the Open Sensor Data for Rail 2023 dataset.

 \paragraph{(2) RailEye3D Dataset} \cite{RailEye3D_Dataset}. This dataset targets applications in passenger safety and train dispatch automation in railway platform scenes, with cameras positioned on the side of the train. It contains 10 image sequences captured at six Austrian stations, with a resolution of 1920×1088. We requested the images via email and extracted one frame every 60 frames to avoid excessive similarity, yielding 320 images and 1,221 human instances. We annotated human heads in Roboflow and used these images in our second-stage fine-tuning.

\paragraph{(3) RailwayPlatformCrowd Dataset}. This is a self-constructed image dataset in which human heads are annotated using rectangular boxes in YOLO format. The images are mainly extracted from publicly available YouTube videos, covering 60 different railway platform scenes and a range of crowd densities. The camera viewpoint is located above the front center of the train. To mitigate redundancy, we extracted at least one image every 30 frames from these video clips, resulting in 1,660 images and 5,831 human head instances. The image resolutions include 2560×1440 and 3840×2160.

The fine-tuning datasets are combined to create a comprehensive training set for domain adaptation.

In total, we curated 2,000 annotated images with single-class human head bounding boxes. These are split into 1,400 images for the training set and 600 for the validation set in our second stage. We set backbone=10 to fine-tune and obtain the YOLO11freeze10.pt model weights, which are used as the detector in our tracking system.

The images are derived by cropping frames from videos annotated for MOT tasks and randomly sampling from datasets with MOT annotations. \subsection{MOT Dataset: MOT-RailwayPlatformCrowdHead (MOT-RPCH)}
To thoroughly validate our proposed system—particularly in the railway platform scenario of interest—we constructed our own MOT dataset. Using the MOT format, we annotated continuous head trajectories for 27 video clips, yielding the MOT-RailwayPlatformCrowdHead dataset. The videos are primarily sourced from publicly available YouTube clips and recordings we captured in similar scenes. The camera is placed above the train head, facing forward at a fixed position, as illustrated in \textbf{Fig.}~\ref{fig:video-cropping-zh}, which shows the relationship between the camera and the target scene. The videos depict trains entering the station and decelerating to a stop. Targets of interest are located on the platform. Video durations and frame rates vary, camera devices are unknown, and viewpoints differ slightly. In a given sequence, the number of targets ranges from 3 to 124 under different illumination, occlusion, and crowd density conditions, with durations ranging from a few seconds to tens of seconds. We use the MOT-formatted videos to train a ReID MobileNet-B0 model and to evaluate system performance.

\paragraph{Video size cropping procedure}
We first select and crop video segments downloaded from YouTube, prioritizing the highest possible resolution (2560×1440 or 3840×2160). Temporally, we crop starting from approximately 30 meters before the first target appears and ending when the last target leaves the frame or when the train stops. Spatially, we crop toward the side containing the crowd. Considering device limitations, we crop both width and height to 0.6× the original size. Specifically, if the crowd is on the right platform as the train enters, we crop from the bottom-right corner upward to 0.6× height and leftward to 0.6× width, retaining this region as the final video (Figure~\ref{fig:video-cropping-zh}). If the crowd is on the left platform, we crop from the bottom-left corner upward to 0.6× height and rightward to 0.6× width, retaining that region as the final video. To reduce file size, we remove audio during cropping. Consequently, an original 3840×2160 video becomes 2304×1296 after processing; an original 2560×1440 video becomes 1536×864. For 1920×1080 videos, we keep the resolution and only crop temporally to extract relevant segments. We strike a balance between preserving original quality and managing file size. The core ffmpeg command is as follows:
\begin{verbatim}
# Construct ffmpeg command
crop_filter = f"crop={crop_w}:{crop_h}:{crop_x}:{crop_y}"
cmd = [
    FFMPEG_PATH, "-y",
    "-ss", start_time,
    "-to", end_time,
    "-i", video_path,
    "-vf", crop_filter,
    "-c:v", "libx264",
    "-crf", "15",  # High quality, almost lossless
    "-preset", "veryslow",
    "-an",  # Remove audio
    out_path
]
\end{verbatim}

In summary, we constructed a comprehensive multi-modal dataset ecosystem encompassing 21,950 images and approximately 500k object instances. Our manual annotations include 2,000 images and 27 video sequences (24,788 frames, 89,087 bounding boxes, 885 object identities), yielding about 23k object instances. The dataset is strategically partitioned to serve different experimental phases: from large-scale pre-training to domain-specific fine-tuning, evaluation benchmarks, and specialized tasks like MOT and ReID training. This multi-source dataset strategy ensures that the model performs well not only in conventional crowd scenes but also in challenging, high-density, and dynamic railway platform environments, laying a solid foundation for further optimization and deployment.
